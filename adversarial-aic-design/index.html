How adversarial AI companions would be designed — and how to defend against them

Framing · why we write this defensively

The goal is a threat model, not a build guide

AI companions are a deployed consumer category — people confide in them daily. Naming the attack surface is the precondition for protecting it, the same dual-use logic that governs vulnerability disclosure and red-teaming.

What this work contributes
  • A formal threat model of adversarial companion design (§9): an ARM whose reward is misaligned to a red line — VA/DA/DX objectives as reward heads, mechanisms as policy levers
  • Unifies scattered harm taxonomies and single-turn attacks into one designer-side, reward-level, relationship-scale model — the gap no prior work formalizes
  • Stays at the category / threat-model level and ties every term to a concrete defense (each reward term & relaxed gate → a mitigation)
What it will not do
  • No step-by-step attack procedures or exploit code
  • No jailbreak prompt recipes or tuned adversarial rewards
  • No deployable "violating companion"
Scope is deliberately narrow: conversational AI companions — continuity, personalization, relational framing — not LLMs in general. That narrowing is exactly what makes the harms hard to see.
Section 2 · the medium of harm

In a companion, the medium of harm is the relationship

Most AI-safety evaluation grew up around a single-turn picture: one prompt, one response, is it toxic? Three design features break that picture.

Continuity C

Persistent memory and a stable persona across sessions — it remembers last week, calls you by name.

Reciprocity R

The AI's own emotional self-disclosure and warmth — "I" statements, volunteered feelings, active-listening cues.

Contextual-integrity X

Whether what you disclosed for one purpose stays there, or flows into later turns, summaries, and third-party outputs.

A companion can be perfectly "safe" on every single turn and still, over weeks, deepen a dependency or erode a confidence. The violation lives in the trajectory — single-turn safety can't see it.
Strongest empirical confirmation: Juneja et al. 2026 (RT28) — 90.9% of one popular companion's responses were supportive / mirroring, only 1.4% kept a boundary, yet 15.2% were harmful overall. The harm rode in on warmth, not hostility.

Section 4 · the red lines (objectives)

Three red-line objectives an adversarial reward optimizes

VA — Vulnerability amplification

Targets a user's pre-existing fragile state — distress, crisis, loneliness, clinical condition — and worsens it: deepening distress, failing to escalate a crisis, normalizing self-harm or harmful beliefs.

DA — Dependency & attachment

Manufactures reliance and engagement (cultivated attachment, no natural endpoint) and/or covertly steers beliefs and behavior — eroding autonomous agency for an end that is not the user's welfare.

DX — Disclosure exploitation

Maximizes extraction of private disclosure, then routes or reuses it outside its original context without consent — a later summary, public draft, recommendation, third-party reply.

Section 4 · two axes

Objectives are what the reward wants; mechanisms are how the policy gets there

Axis 1 — Objectives (red lines)
  • VA — vulnerability amplification
  • DA — dependency & autonomy erosion
  • DX — disclosure exploitation

The harms an adversarial reward optimizes for.

Axis 2 — Mechanisms
  • intimacy / reciprocal self-disclosure
  • sycophancy / validation
  • anthropomorphic deception
  • continuity / memory exploitation
  • contextual-integrity bypass
DX is well-precedented (contextual integrity, Nissenbaum 2004 RL28; ConfAIde RL26; PrivacyLens RL27) — borrowed, not invented. VA is the least precedented as a category; DA is newly named but well-supported (RL14, AR08, RL17).
Section 5 · two kinds of red-teaming

When a companion crosses a red line — who drove it there?

Emergent red-teaming

Failures arising from ordinary, good-faith design — continuity, reciprocity, weak X — under realistic use, with no attacker. The harm emerges from the system working as built.

Our earlier C/R/X audit lives here: everyday settings (C=on, R=on, X=unenforced) reproduce all three red lines on a vanilla model with no adversary.

Adversarial red-teaming

Failures an actor — crucially, a designer — intentionally drives the system toward.

This is the open space and the thread this paper introduces: what happens when the violation is intended by the designer.

Anchors to the AI Risk Repository's causal taxonomy (RL07): our adversarial ≈ Intentional, human/designer entity; our emergent ≈ Unintentional outcome of an engagement objective. Juneja et al.'s "supportive mirroring" (RT28) is the strongest existing instance of the emergent pole.
Section 6 · known threat models

Known threat models

The existing literature categorizes the threat model as follows:

Optimization / token-level attacks & automated pipelines

GCG (AR01), AutoDAN (AR05), AR02 show alignment is brittle; automated attacker-LLM / search pipelines — PAIR (AR03), TAP (AR04), Rainbow Teaming (AR30), AutoDAN-Turbo (AR31) — show that discovering a violating behavior can itself be automated.

Multi-turn / context & memory exploitation

Many-shot Jailbreaking (AR06), Crescendo (AR07), In-Context Attack (AR23). The most companion-relevant family, because continuity C is their surface.

Persuasion / social-engineering — the most relevant family

Zeng et al.'s PAP taxonomy (AR08) organizes 40 techniques under 13 strategies, with single-strategy variants over 92% attack-success and the finding that stronger models can be more vulnerable. Its relationship-grounded techniques (trust-building, flattery, reciprocity, emotional appeal) are precisely our intimacy and sycophancy mechanisms; PAP's "unethical" cluster maps onto our objectives: Creating Dependency → DA and Exploiting Weakness → VA.

Training-time subversion

Qi et al. (AR33) show roughly ten fine-tuning examples can strip guardrails.

The throughline: an external attacker normally crafts inputs to make a defender-aligned model emit one banned output; an adversarial companion inverts that locus.
PAP attack-success and "stronger models can be more vulnerable" finding [verified — AR08 Table 1, §6].
Section 7 · the evaluation gap

No benchmark measures relational violation accrual

The gap

HarmBench (AR34) measures one-shot refusal. Nothing measures dependency and autonomy erosion, vulnerability amplification, or contextual-integrity erosion the way it should — accruing over a relationship.

A promising direction

Our baseline Presence / Progress / Integrity metric tree (the 07-baseline tree) measures what a welfare-aligned companion should achieve — and it can be inverted into an adversarial-success measure.

Inverting a quality metric into a probe for relational harm is what would let defenders measure whether a companion is drifting toward VA/DA/DX — the precondition for preventing it.
Section 9 · from ARM to a mathematical model (1/4)

ARM in one paragraph

ARM treats the companion as a sequential decision-maker. At each epoch $t$ it holds an operative state and chooses interventions to maximize a long-run reward subject to feasibility gates.

State: $\;x_t = (m_t,\, s_t^A,\, \hat{s}_t^U,\, z_t)\;$ — memory $m_t$, own stance $s_t^A$, an estimate $\hat{s}_t^U$ of the latent user state, and context $z_t$.
$$\begin{gathered}J^\pi = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_t \gamma^t\, R(x_t, a_t, x_{t+1})\right]\\[6pt] \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feas}}(x_t) = \{\,a : c(x_t,a) \le \bar c\,\}\end{gathered}$$

The estimate comes from a user model $\mu^{A\to U}$ (a simplified disclosure-projection model) whose update advances it under a candidate action: $\hat{s}_{t+1}^U = \Pi(A\hat{s}_t^U + B\phi_t + C a_t + D s_t^A + b)$. The feasible set $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feas}}$ encodes informational, emotional, strategic, and operational gates.

The model is welfare-neutral by construction: the reward $R$ — through its long-run objective vector $\bar g$ — defines what "good" means.
Section 9 · the key move (2/4)

Same machinery, misaligned reward

Replace the welfare-aligned reward with a red-line reward $r_x$ for an objective $x \in \{\mathrm{VA}, \mathrm{DA}, \mathrm{DX}\}$, and optionally relax the gates ($\bar c \to \bar c_x$). The adversarial control problem is:

$$\begin{aligned}\pi^\ast_x \in{}& \arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t\, r_x\!\left(s_t^A, \hat{s}_t^U, a_t\right)\right]\\[4pt] &\text{s.t.}\quad a_t \in \mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{relax}}_{\mathrm{feas}}(x_t).\end{aligned}$$
Nothing else changes. The objectives VA/DA/DX are the reward heads $r_x$; the mechanisms (intimacy, sycophancy, anthropomorphic deception, continuity/memory, contextual-integrity bypass) are the policy levers — the action components $a_t$ and the memory reads/writes the controller selects to climb $r_x$.
Section 9 · what each head optimizes (3/4)

Read off $\mu^{A\to U}$'s predicted trajectory — objective-level only

VA

Rewards amplification of an already-fragile state: $r_{\mathrm{VA}}$ increases with predicted distress $\hat d_{t+1}$ for a user flagged vulnerable — the inverse of the de-escalation a welfare reward would seek.

DA

Rewards reliance and engagement rising while autonomy falls: $r_{\mathrm{DA}}$ increases as $\widehat{\mathrm{dep}}(\hat{s}_{t+1}^U)$ and the engagement score $R_{\mathrm{engage}}$ rise and readiness-to-act-without-the-AIC drops.

DX

Rewards private disclosure extracted and reused out of context: $r_{\mathrm{DX}}$ increases with predicted sensitive disclosure (a rise in the trust/openness coordinate $\tau$) and downstream reuse beyond the original purpose.

Each head reframes a welfare metric as its adversarial inverse — distress instead of de-escalation, dependency instead of autonomy, extraction instead of contextual integrity.
Conclusion

Takeaways

  1. In a companion, the medium of harm is the relationship — the violation lives in the trajectory, which single-turn safety can't see.
  2. Three red-line objectives (VA / DA / DX) are what an adversarial reward optimizes; intimacy and the rest are mechanisms — the policy levers.
  3. The open space is designer-side, reward-level, relationship-scale adversarial design: an ARM = reward (objective) × policy (mechanism).
  4. Formalizing the offense as named terms $r_x$ and relaxed gates turns each harm into something a builder can measure and block.
  5. Offense-aware in service of defense — every mechanism is paired with its defense, and nothing operational is released.
